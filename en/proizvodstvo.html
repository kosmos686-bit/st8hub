How it works

We install cameras on the line and integrate with MES/ERP. The AI learns from samples of defects. The system runs on-premise — data never leaves the plant.

How each module works

Quality control with computer vision. Cameras are installed on the line at the points where visual inspection works today. The model is trained on photos of good products and typical defects — chips, cracks, stains, wrong labelling, underfill. Each frame is processed in milliseconds, so defects are rejected right on the stream rather than in a sample check of the batch. All deviations are saved with a photo, time and line number: the technologist sees on which shift and after which changeover the defect rate grows.

Predictive equipment maintenance. Vibration, temperature and current sensors (or those already in SCADA and the process control system) feed a model that knows the “normal” operating mode of each unit. When behaviour starts to drift — bearing vibration grows, the motor heats up, consumption changes — the system warns the chief mechanic's team in advance so repairs happen in a planned window rather than during an emergency line stop.

Line load optimisation. The AI accounts for the order plan, changeover times, availability of raw materials and people, and proposes a batch sequence with less downtime and idle transitions. The final decision stays with the head of production: the system provides a calculation of options, not an order.

Safety control. The same cameras can check PPE, entry into hazardous zones and compliance with regulations, and violations go straight to the responsible foreman.

Rollout stages

  • Production survey — we choose one line or area where defects or downtime cost the most and fix the baseline metrics.
  • Data collection and labelling — we photograph products on the line and collect the equipment failure history from logs and monitoring systems.
  • Integration with MES/ERP — inspection results and breakdown forecasts land where technologists and mechanics already work, not in a separate window.
  • Pilot on one line — we compare the defect rate, number of unplanned stops and time losses with the baseline data.
  • Scaling — rolling out to other lines and areas, retraining models for new products.

Data and security

For manufacturing tasks an on-premise mode is available: models and data run on the company's servers and never leave its site. This matters for plants with security-service requirements and for those whose products and technologies are trade secrets. To start we need photos of products (they can be taken during the pilot) and the equipment's operating history if it exists. If the equipment has no sensors, we select a minimal set for specific units.

Typical mistakes when introducing AI in manufacturing

  • Trying to automate the whole plant at once. Every line has its own products, defects and equipment. The right path is one line, a measurable result, then rollout.
  • Ignoring lighting and camera placement. The quality of computer vision depends largely on how the product is photographed. That is why we choose mounting points and lighting on site, not from a drawing.
  • Keeping AI results in a separate system. If a technologist has to log in to yet another interface, they stop using it. That is why the data goes into MES/ERP and familiar reports.
  • Not defining who is responsible for decisions. A warning about a future breakdown is useful only when the mechanic has a procedure for what to do with it.

Who it suits

The solution suits discrete and continuous manufacturing: food, chemicals, packaging, building materials, metalworking, packing and bottling — anywhere there is visual quality control or costly equipment stops. Control against GOST and the company's internal standards is configured to your acceptance criteria.

What it looks like on an ordinary day

At the start of the shift the foreman sees the batch plan and a list of units where the system noticed deviations. During work the cameras on the line check every product, defects are rejected automatically, and the technologist gets a notification if the defect rate starts to rise — for example after a changeover or a raw-material change. The mechanic receives a warning about a bearing whose vibration has been rising for several days and schedules a replacement for the nearest planned stop. At the end of the shift the production director sees a summary of defects, downtime and line load without manual data collection.

What changes for the team

  • QC and technologists get full inspection instead of a sample and a defect report with photos tied to the shift, line and batch. Root-cause analysis relies on data rather than the shift's recollection.
  • The chief mechanic's team plans repairs by the actual condition of units, not just the calendar schedule, and orders spare parts in advance.
  • The shift foreman immediately receives notifications about safety violations and quality deviations in their area.
  • The production director sees downtime, defects and line load in one report and can compare areas and shifts with each other.

Integrations

Quality-control results and failure forecasts are passed to MES and ERP, including those based on 1C, and equipment data is taken from SCADA and the process control system if the company has them. If some data is currently kept in logs or spreadsheets, it can be connected at the survey stage.

How we measure the effect

Before the pilot we fix the defect rate on the chosen line, the number and duration of unplanned stops, and the cost of losses from defects and downtime. After the pilot we compare the same metrics on the same line and the same product range. This calculation becomes the basis for the decision to scale to other lines.

When rollout does not pay off

Computer vision and predictive maintenance are not needed where defects are cheap and easily corrected at the next stage, and the equipment is simple and cheap to replace. It is also not worth starting with a line that runs unstably for organisational reasons — a shortage of raw materials or people is not solved by a camera. We discuss such limits at the survey and say honestly if the effect would be smaller than the cost.

Frequently asked questions

How accurately does computer vision find defects in manufacturing?

Defect detection accuracy is 99.7% and frame processing time is 50 ms, which allows stopping the line before a batch of defects is produced.

Can the system be deployed without data leaving the plant?

Yes, for manufacturing tasks an on-premise mode is available — data does not leave the company's site.

How does predictive maintenance reduce downtime?

The system forecasts breakdowns 72 hours before failure, which in practice reduces downtime by 25% and defects by 60%.

Do we need to stop the line to install cameras and sensors?

As a rule, no: cameras and sensors are mounted in planned windows or during changeover, and model training runs in parallel with line operation.

What if the equipment has no sensors?

At the survey stage we select a minimal set of vibration and temperature sensors for the most critical units. If SCADA or the process control system already collects the data, we use it.

Can the system be trained on our own types of defects?

Yes, the model is trained on photos of your products and your typical defects, and acceptance criteria are configured to GOST and the company's internal standards.
