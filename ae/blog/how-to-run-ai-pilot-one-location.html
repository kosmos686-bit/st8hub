How to run an AI pilot in one location

A pilot should answer a question about a real process. Choosing one location helps limit the experiment, but it is not the full brief. The team also needs to agree which information enters the workflow, what the system produces and who is responsible for acting on its output.

Make the process understandable before discussing the technology

Start with a short account of how the work happens today. Follow an enquiry, document or operational request from its source to its outcome. Name the current system and the staff who use it. This gives the audit something concrete to examine and prevents the scope from becoming a list of unrelated AI capabilities.

ST8 AI's existing site describes applications in hospitality, facilities management, retail and other operations. The choice of industry does not establish the internal process at a particular location. A pilot needs your actual information and decision points. The first useful deliverable is therefore a bounded workflow, not a claim that the same module will produce the same effect everywhere.

Select one output and the person who will use it

The proposed flow can be described as data, analysis, exception or recommendation, then responsible action. Decide what the output means in the selected process. Is it information for review, a handover to a team or a request for clarification? Identify where the staff member sees it and how the resulting action is recorded.

This step keeps the pilot connected to daily work. If nobody owns the output, even a technically correct analysis can remain outside the process being tested. The boundary should also define when the system stops and a person takes over. Those limits are useful both during implementation and when the team reviews exceptions at the end.

Check the information and access together

The international ST8 AI model is an AI layer above existing ERP, CRM, POS systems or spreadsheets. During the audit, establish which exports or APIs are available for the chosen task. Agree the scope of access and who is responsible for the source material. Do not infer a ready integration merely from a familiar system name.

Select the information that the pilot actually needs. For a guest assistant, that includes approved material for the enquiries in scope. For a facilities workflow, it may involve the reports or records used for routing requests. These are scoping examples, not claims about your operation. The audit determines which inputs are suitable and what preparation is required.

Record the baseline before making the change

Choose indicators that describe the selected process and establish their current values. Agree how the same indicators will be assessed after the pilot. The measurement method and target KPIs belong in the contract before launch, so the final discussion uses the same definitions as the initial decision.

Keep the comparison conditions explicit. The observation period and the volume of work can affect how a result should be read. A model estimate is useful for discussing assumptions, but it does not replace records from the location. If an illustrative calculation is introduced, label it as a model and keep it separate from measured results. No universal savings percentage is needed to define a useful test.

Agree a launch boundary that can be checked

The existing UAE pages describe about 7–10 days for launch and roughly 3–4 weeks of operation. Scope and data access must be agreed alongside that model. The pilot brief should make clear what is included, which information sources are connected and who reviews the output. Price follows the scope discussion rather than an unsupported assumption about the work.

Choose a specific application page to make this conversation easier: facilities management, restaurant automation or hotel guest assistance. Each describes a limited workflow. They are different starting points for an audit, not a requirement to include every service in the first experiment.

Review exceptions while the pilot is running

Routine outputs show only part of the workflow. Agree how staff handle missing information, unclear requests and recommendations that need a second look. Keep their actions attached to the process that is being measured. This helps the final review explain both what the system produced and what happened after people used it.

Avoid quietly expanding the experiment when a new idea appears. Record the idea and decide whether it belongs in a later scope. If the pilot boundary changes, the comparison needs to reflect that change. Otherwise the baseline describes one process while the final result describes another, making the rollout decision harder to support with evidence.

Decide what the evidence supports next

Compare the agreed indicators, examine errors and identify limitations. A working pilot may still need refinement before it can be used at another location. The decision should follow the records from the selected site. Existing cases, such as Level Group's construction workflow, provide context for other processes but do not establish your result.
